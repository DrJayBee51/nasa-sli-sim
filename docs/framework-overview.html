How the pieces fit together

Information flow

Why run two simulators at all?

The honest version

Because the handbook asks us to — and because it makes our numbers harder to argue with. Mission Performance Predictions, repeated in the PDR, CDR and FRR, asks teams to “present data from a different calculation method,” to “discuss any differences,” and to “perform multiple simulations to verify that results are precise.”

Most teams answer that last one by running OpenRocket five times with different wind speeds. That shows the software is repeatable. It says nothing about whether the rocket is. We answer it by admitting what we don't know, sampling it a thousand times, and reporting the odds.

Genuinely independent
  • 01Flight dynamics — two different integrators solving two different formulations
  • 02Centre of pressure — each works it out from its own description of the rocket
  • 03Fin lift — Barrowman versus Diederich; genuinely different theories
  • 04Atmosphere, wind and event detection
  • 05Descent solver — different schemes entirely
Shared, by design
  • —The motor thrust curve
  • —Mass, centre of gravity and inertia
  • —The drag coefficient — RocketPy has no drag model of its own; it has to be told

So the two engines agreeing does not prove our drag estimate is right. If OpenRocket is 15% low, both are 15% low together — and they agree beautifully while both being wrong. That is exactly why we fly, and why the amber loop exists.

What we admit we don't know

The dispersions

A single simulation answers “what happens if every number is exactly right?” — a flight that will never occur. So before we run anything, we write down every quantity we're unsure about and how unsure we are. Those sixteen numbers are the honest part of the model, and they're worth more scrutiny than the simulation itself.

The airframe

Dry mass±1.5% Epoxy, paint, wiring and fillets never weigh what the CAD said.
CG position±1 cm Moves with build tolerance and where the payload actually sits.
Drag coefficient±7% The big one, and the one we can shrink by flying.

The motor

Total impulse±2% Batch-to-batch variation in the propellant. Nothing we can do about it.
Burn time±1.5% Held independent of impulse, so the two don't double-count.
Casing mass±1% Hardware tolerance, plus whatever residue stays after burn.

Launch day

Wind speed±2.2 m/s Never below zero, never above 27 mph — past that the RSO scrubs.
Wind direction±35° Around whatever the forecast says for the field that month.
Rail angle5° ± 1.5° The RSO sets this on the day. We don't get to choose it.
Rail heading0–360° Genuinely unknown relative to the wind, so it's drawn flat.
Air temperature±6 K Changes air density, which changes every aerodynamic force.
Air pressure±900 Pa Same story — density again.

Recovery

Main parachute Cd±10% Canopy drag at this scale is genuinely poorly known.
Drogue Cd±10% Worse, because the vehicle is half-tumbling under it.
Main deploy altitude±25 ft Altimeter accuracy plus how the bay is vented.
Drogue delay±0.25 s Bounded so the total can never break the 2-second rule.

Read those three panels together. Apogee is a motor and drag problem. Where it lands is almost entirely a rail angle problem — a number the range officer picks, not us. And landing speed is a parachute problem, full stop. Three requirements, three different things to worry about.

One thing to take away

Two things dominate our apogee: the motor and the drag. We cannot do anything about the motor — that scatter is baked in at the factory. Drag we can do something about. Before we fly it's a 7% guess; after the demonstration flight it's a 2–3% measurement, and the apogee spread roughly halves.

So we don't improve our altitude score by simulating harder. We improve it by flying, measuring, and feeding the answer back in.
